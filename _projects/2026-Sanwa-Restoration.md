I grew up riding bikes, and love the freedom they give. When in Austin for a summer internship, I needed a way to get around the city, so I decided to get a project bike I could work on and ride. After a close inspection in person, I bought a 1980s Sanwa Technique off of Facebook Marketplace.

I also started volunteering with The Yellow Bike Project in Austin, where if you get enough volunteer hours, you can restore a bike with their shop and parts for free. A bill of materials can be found at the bottom of this page with all used and new parts I either bought or scavenged for free.

As I was looking through bins and bins of old parts, I found a huge variety of designs for parts with the same function. This lead me to research the differences, and to explore why certain decisions were made, and what made some more effective than others. Some of my findings can be found in the side notes below.

### Wheels, Tires, and Pedals

<figure style="float: left; width: 38%; min-width: 200px; margin: 0 20px 15px 0; text-align: center;">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/Pedals.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Rockbros Pedals" style="width: 100%; border-radius: 6px; box-shadow: 2px 2px 6px rgba(0,0,0,0.1);">
  <figcaption style="font-size: 0.9em; color: #666; margin-top: 8px;"><em>New Rockbros flat pedals</em></figcaption>

After an initial cleaning and assessment of the bike, I decided to start with the wheels. I bought two new tubes, and found two used Bontrager tires. I had a little bit of space to increase the tire width from 25 mm to 32 mm. Though the rims were in true, I noticed the back axle was bent, and that both rims were quite hefty. I decided to replace them both and upgrade from a six-speed to an eight-speed rear cassette.

I found two different used 700c rims in the back and trued them. I then found an eight-speed cassette to fit on the back wheel. It took a lot of searching and trial and error to find a cassette combo that worked. Though the new rims were 130mm wide, my bike frame was only about 126mm. Because I have a steel frame, I bend the back chainstay and seatstay bars to jam a wider rim in. See the side note below on frame material properties for more info (coming soon).

Another initial fix were the pedals. The old pedals were very small, and only had tread on one side, which made pedaling difficult. I found black Rockbros flat pedals that were double-sided, and replaced the old ones.

### Wheel Side Note

When looking at the hundreds of used wheels in the back, I saw a lot of different spoke patterns. The three most common are shown below, radial, 2-cross, and 3-cross. I've got a radial front wheel and a 2-cross back wheel. The cross patterns are how many spokes each spoke crosses between the rim and the hub. Spokes are connected to the hub tangentially in this pattern. Each cross increases the lateral rigidity of the wheel, and increases its ability to withstand torsional loads.

The radial pattern involves no spoke crossing, which means less spokes overall and therefore a lighter wheel, but also decreased torsional strength. Since the spokes are perpendicular to the hub, this pattern leads to a radially stiffer ride, as the load is transferred more directly to your hands, and not dissipated rotationally.

### Drivetrain Tuning

<figure style="float: right; width: 30%; min-width: 250px; margin: 0 0 15px 20px; text-align: center;">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/Limiting_Screw.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Derailleur Limiting Screws" style="width: 80%; border-radius: 6px; box-shadow: 2px 2px 6px rgba(0,0,0,0.1);">
  <figcaption style="font-size: 0.9em; color: #666; margin-top: 8px;"><em>High Limit Screw Loosened All the Way</em></figcaption>

After cleaning the chain in a coffee can and replacing the shifting cables and shifting cable housing, I began slowly tuning the friction shifters. The first thing I noticed was that the chain was rubbing against the derailleur in the highest gears. I removed a link to pull the bottom part of the derailleur down, which created some clearance between the chain and the derailleur.

As this bike was originally a 6-speed, the derailleur is not entirely compatible with the new cassette, and so the highest gear is still a little bit sticky. You can see that the limiting screw is loosened all the way, which isn't ideal. In the future, I might upgrade the derailleur.

### Crankset Side Note

The original Biopace crankset is actually an ovalish shape, not circular, which changes the mechanical advantage of the gear depending on where you are in the stroke, smoothing out the pedaling motion. The effective radius is smaller during the power stroke (where the cranks are horizontal), and larger during the "dead zone" (where the cranks are vertical).

This allows you to build momentum during the power stroke where you have a lower gear and carry it through the dead zone, reducing the strain on your knees during the change of direction and smoothing out the stroke.

This is the opposite of some other crankset designs, which give you the lower gear during the dead spot, and the higher gear during the power stroke. This other idea was sound in theory, as it gives you a higher gear in the spot where you can generate more power, but was ineffective in practice and led to heightened knee pain.

### Brakes and Handlebars

    <!-- Text Section -->
    <p>I wanted to change to black handlebar wrap and get new cabling for the brakes, so I began by removing the original white handlebar wrap and hoods. The white wrap gets dirty too fast and was starting to degrade due to its age.</p>
    
    <p>I then tried to put on new brake lever hoods, but they ended up being too small and ripped down the middle. I thought about just using extra black wrap to go around the exposed lever, but decided to go the extra mile and replace the levers entirely.</p>
    
    <p>I ended up finding some sweet Origin8 brake levers with a white and black colorway that went nicely with my bike. My original brake calipers are not Origin8, however, so I made sure to test ride without the wrap on to make sure the braking power was adequate. The ratio of caliper movement to lever movement is different across different models, and a mismatch can result in extremely tough braking.</p>
    
    <p>Fortunately, the ratio of these levers worked with my calipers. After tuning the brakes, I finished off the handlebars with fresh black wrap and I'm super happy with how everything turned out.</p>
